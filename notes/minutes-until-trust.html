Where my time went after the agent wrote the code

From opening a pull request to merging it, my median was 95 minutes for changes with one review round and 351 minutes for changes with seven or more. I counted 77 changes in the first group and 19 in the second. How much of that time needed my attention?

I went back to the timestamps from my coding sessions. They showed me where to look, but they couldn’t tell me when I was reading a diff, fixing a mistake, or working on something else. I’d fund a rollout if the value of staff time saved per accepted change exceeded the added agent and infrastructure costs, and the team stayed within its limits for rework, defects and time to merge.

ithe question

How many minutes did a change need from me?

In my study of automated reviewers, a review arrived 3.9 minutes after a push at the median. A reader described a different clock he keeps by hand: from the moment he opens a diff until he trusts the change, including any second read. Another asked where an engineer’s own time appears alongside the agent’s bill [1, 2].

When I tested a filter for code reviews, 105 documentation blocks qualified to skip. Across 20 runs, every change still needed review. In the discussion that followed, an engineer picked out both facts that mattered to him: the sample was small and zero whole reviews went away. A product manager saw a reason to start with a small pilot and test how the workflow changes. That left me with a question I could put to my own team. If someone still has to open the change, what work does the agent actually take off their hands?

I can wait for a test while I review another change. I can also spend ten uninterrupted minutes figuring out why a passing test missed a bug. Both look like ten minutes on a delivery dashboard. Only one of them took ten minutes of my attention away from other work.

iithe session record

What the 35 and 532 hours actually counted

My October 24 summary recorded 35.14 hours in a field called “specify” and 532.18 hours in one called “review” across 9,111 sessions. To interpret those labels, I checked the calculation in use at the time.

October totalStart of intervalEnd of interval
35 hoursFirst recorded session eventFirst tool call
532 hoursLast tool callLast recorded session event

The calculation caps each interval at 30 minutes per session. A session without a tool call contributes zero to both fields. I might review the first diff and redirect the agent before its next tool call. That work falls between the two intervals. Conversely, a long gap after the final tool call can count while my attention is elsewhere. Calling the first total “prompting” and the second “reviewing and steering” would put an activity label on time the logs cannot identify.

When I rechecked the warehouse, 7,136 rows still carried timestamps through the original cutoff. Of these, 797 reached the 30-minute cap after the final tool call. Those sessions accounted for 88.9 percent of the 448.2 hours in that interval in the surviving rows. The full October totals above come from the original summary. This later check shows how strongly the cap affects the subset I could reproduce.

Where a change waited

I followed 182 merged code changes in one repository from October 6 to 18. I counted 504 review rounds. I could group 174 changes by round count. The medians rise across all five groups. More complex changes may need more rounds and take longer anyway, so I can’t use the difference to predict what removing a round would save.

Once the final checks were green and the review had landed, another 29 minutes elapsed at the median before the change entered the merge queue. That interval includes time when the change could sit unattended. For a pilot I’d track it alongside a reviewer’s own record of time spent on the change.

ivcorrections

Which failures deserve a closer look?

After the correction study, readers asked how much time different mistakes cost me [3, 4]. In the October sample I could match 144 actionable correction labels to 87 sessions. Of those labels, 89 (61.8 percent) described failures to finish, verify the work, or leave it in a usable state.

I divided each session’s two recorded intervals equally among its actionable labels. Execution failures then accounted for 478.2 of 672.5 allocated minutes (71.1 percent). A session with two labels contributed half its minutes to each, even if one correction took me much longer. I’d start by observing fixes for execution failures in the pilot. To price a fix, I’d need someone to record when the work began and ended.

vtask length

Why I’d include long runs in the pilot

In the October summary, 2,936 of the 5,311 sessions with debriefs and a positive timing measure had at least 40 assistant turns. Those sessions accounted for 84.8 percent of the combined time before the first and after the last tool call. That concentration makes long runs worth sampling. It leaves open whether splitting a long run would make a change easier to ship.

One reader described mistakes compounding on longer tasks. Another reported more review work as his agent filled its context window [5, 6]. My comparison groups sessions by turn count. Testing either explanation would require recording when a mistake appeared, how much context the agent had used and how long a person spent correcting it.

vithe funding decision

What would justify a rollout?

I’d run the candidate agent alongside the team’s current workflow on comparable changes. Before work starts, I’d group tasks by type and complexity, then randomly assign tasks within each group. Both groups would use the same acceptance checks. Engineers would record the minutes they actually spend prompting, reading diffs, correcting work and verifying it, including time spent on attempts that never ship.

Take a reviewer who opens a diff, reads it, requests a fix and comes back after the tests pass. I’d ask them to time both reads and the work of explaining the fix, pausing when they switch tasks. I’d keep the test wait on the delivery clock. For a first pilot I’d keep the review rules and required checks fixed and record their versions. If the team also wanted to change a rule, I’d test that separately so I could tell which change earned its cost.

For each group I’d divide those total staff minutes by the number of accepted changes. I’d price the difference at the team’s loaded labor rate and compare it with the additional agent and infrastructure cost per accepted change. I’d also track time to merge, rework and defects discovered after release, using the same follow-up period for both groups.

In the discussion of the review filter, a founder also asked about the cost of missed issues. I’d include staff time spent recovering from those issues during follow-up in both groups’ totals, and record any additional operational impact separately. A fix that takes another engineer an afternoon belongs in the cost of that accepted change.

My rollout decision

I’d expand only if the value of staff time saved per accepted change exceeded the added cost.

Before the pilot I’d agree with the team on acceptable limits for rework, post-release defects and delivery time. A rollout would also have to stay within those limits. If the sample were too small to tell whether it cleared either test, I’d keep the pilot limited.
